How to Read a Cardiology Randomized Trial

An educational guide to how to Read a Cardiology Randomized Trial, focused on research literacy rather than hype.

2026-10-10 14 min read Intermediate Both

Key takeaways

  • Start with the clinical question (PICO), then eligibility—who was enrolled defines who the answer applies to.
  • Primary endpoint hierarchy is prespecified in the protocol; secondary endpoints are exploratory unless trial powered for them.
  • Intention-to-treat analysis preserves randomization; per-protocol analyses can bias toward efficacy.
  • Report both relative effect (HR, RR) and absolute event rates with confidence intervals—NNT derives from absolute differences.
  • Guideline adoption tracks consistency across trials, safety, and feasibility—not a single positive p-value.

What problem the research is trying to solve

Randomized trials exist to estimate causal effects of interventions while minimizing confounding. Cardiology trials influence stent choices, drug labels, and device approvals worldwide.

Readers without biostatistics training can still master a structured approach that catches most misleading claims.

The problem is information overload: supplementary appendices hold randomization details, event adjudication, and subgroup plans.

This article is a reusable reading framework, not commentary on one trial.

How studies in this area are usually designed

Parallel-group RCTs dominate: eligible patients randomized to intervention vs control, followed until prespecified events accrue.

Event-driven designs extend follow-up until target event counts—explains why trial duration varies by control event rate.

Crossover, factorial, and cluster designs appear in cardiology—identify design before interpreting statistics.

Registry-linked pragmatic trials blend real-world data with randomization—watch for loss to follow-up.

How to read claims critically

Check allocation concealment and blinding where feasible—open-label procedural trials still valid but bias procedures toward intervention.

Composite endpoints: require each component clinically meaningful and similarly serious; inspect component-specific forest plots.

Non-inferiority margins are clinical judgments encoded statistically—superiority claims need HR CI entirely below 1.

Fragility: small changes in a few events can flip significance—consider fragility index alongside p-values.

What is ready for clinics versus still emerging

Trials with consistent mortality or MACE benefit across multiple studies drive ACC/AHA class I recommendations when feasible.

Surrogate-only trials (plaque volume, FFR) may inform research paths without changing standard care immediately.

Negative trials are informative— they narrow eligible populations and stop harmful widespread adoption.

Translation includes cost, training, and equity—not only statistical significance.

Student takeaways and further reading habits

Use a one-page trial worksheet: funding, N, follow-up, primary endpoint, ITT result, harms, applicability.

Practice with landmark trials (4S, COURAGE, DAPA-HF) to calibrate expectations for effect sizes.

Read CONSORT diagrams for dropout patterns.

Cross-check ClinicalTrials.gov registration for outcome switching versus published primary endpoint.

Putting it together

Reading cardiology RCTs is a learnable skill that protects patients and learners from hype.

Absolute effects and applicability questions should close every journal club.

Cardily reinforces methods education; clinical application stays with care teams.
