Understanding Coronary Vasospasm: A Clear Educational Guide

An evidence-aware educational overview of coronary vasospasm, written for students and curious readers seeking clarity without personal medical advice.

2026-10-10 12 min read Intermediate Both

Key takeaways

  • Coronary vasospasm is reversible coronary artery narrowing from smooth muscle constriction, classically linked to Prinzmetal angina.
  • Episodes may occur at rest, often at night, and can produce transient ST elevation on ECG.
  • Functional ischemia can exist with angiographically mild or normal epicardial arteries.
  • Triggers include smoking, stimulants, and certain medications in teaching lists.
  • Diagnosis may require provocative testing in specialty settings— not a self-test topic.

Core definition

Coronary vasospasm narrows the vessel lumen through intense smooth muscle contraction rather than fixed atherosclerotic plaque. When flow drops below myocardial demand, ischemia and pain result, sometimes with arrhythmia.

Prinzmetal (variant) angina historically described rest angina with ST elevation that resolves as spasm relaxes.

Vasospasm coexists with atherosclerosis in some patients; stenosis lowers flow reserve, making spasm more symptomatic.

Misconception: young smokers with normal angiograms cannot have serious ischemia—vasospasm disproves that.

Pathophysiology in plain language

Endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and hypercontractile smooth muscle contribute in models. Smoking is a major preventable contributor in educational materials.

Transmural ischemia during spasm can mimic STEMI on ECG until resolution—differentiation requires clinical correlation and serial ECG.

Multivessel spasm or malignant arrhythmia during spasm explains rare sudden events in case reports.

Microvascular angina may overlap when epicardial spasm is absent but microvascular constriction occurs.

Symptoms and warning patterns

Rest chest pain, especially nocturnal or early morning, with cyclical ST changes supports teaching diagnoses. Exercise may be less triggering than in fixed stenosis, though overlap exists.

Symptoms can include syncope or palpitations when ischemia provokes arrhythmia.

Atypical chest pain workups sometimes miss vasospasm if only exercise stress is performed.

Patient-reported triggers (cold exposure, stress) are clues, not proof.

Tests you will hear about

Coronary angiography with intracoronary acetylcholine or ergonovine provocative testing is used in research and expert centers with safety protocols.

Ambulatory ECG may capture transient ST changes during spontaneous pain.

Non-invasive coronary CT may exclude fixed disease before functional testing emphasis.

Calcium channel blockers and nitrates appear in guideline discussions for treatment—specific prescribing is clinician role.

Learning checkpoints

Contrast vasospasm with fixed stenosis using timing and ECG features in case vignettes.

List modifiable triggers to counsel about in general education (smoking cessation themes).

Explain why exercise stress tests may be falsely reassuring.

Study MINOCA workups where vasospasm enters differential after non-obstructive angiography.

Putting it together

Coronary vasospasm highlights functional ischemia—dynamic luminal change without severe fixed plaque.

Rest pain and transient ST elevation are pedagogical clues; confirmation often needs specialized testing.
